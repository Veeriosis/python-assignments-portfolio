## The Promise of Nuclear Fusion

Despite the ongoing climate crisis, nuclear fusion may hold the key to greener energy production. Subatomic particles called light nuclei are forced together under extreme heat and pressure, releasing immense amounts of energy for conversion into electricity (Chatzis and Barbarino 2021). The energy released by nuclear fusion is millions of times greater than that produced by burning fossil fuels, while not emitting $\text{CO}_2$ or long-lived nuclear waste (Ball 2023). However, current scientific feasibility does not imply economic and technological feasibility. As countries work to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, the question is: can nuclear fusion expand at a sufficient rate to help fight climate change?

## How We Investigated Fusion's Potential

Three computer models were built to estimate how quickly fusion power plants might be developed, the fall of electricity costs as more plants are built, and quantities of $\text{CO}_2$ they could avoid. We examined four possible futures for climate change: countries continuing their current policies, countries fulfilling their climate pledges, stronger climate action beginning after 2030 (to limit warming to below $2^\circ\text{C}$), and ambitious efforts to limit warming to $1.5^\circ\text{C}$ (Byers et al. 2022). Additionally, faster (8-20%) and slower (2-8%) reductions in fusion's electricity cost/number of fusion plants were considered (Tang et al. 2026). All eight combinations were simulated 10,000 times using different plausible assumptions. Our models also accounted for high electricity costs earlier in fusion plant development (Lindley et al. 2023).

## Our Results

Our models projected fusion electricity to contribute $\sim 0\%$ of global electricity by 2050. By 2100, under countries' climate pledges, fusion supplied 1.3% of global electricity with faster cost reductions and 0.3% with slower reductions. Additionally, installation timing was significant. With a faster cost reduction, opening the first commercial plant in 2035 rather than 2055 increased projected emissions avoided from $0.35$ to $4.30$ billion tonnes of $\text{CO}_2$ (Figure 1). The rate at which plants could be deployed had the strongest influence on emissions savings. Furthermore, under slower cost reductions, 68% of simulations did not reach equal costs with alternative low-carbon sources by 2100, compared with $18\%$ under faster reductions.

## What Does This Mean for Our Future?

It is important to acknowledge that model outputs are not confident predictors. Commercialized fusion does not currently operate; therefore, results are uncertain. However, literature provides evidence for scenarios where fusion contributes more before the mid-century (Lerede et al. 2023) in comparison to our predictions.

As for the future, fusion may become an effective long-term source of carbon-minimal electricity, although it is unlikely to be the solution if current climate goals necessitate immediate emission reductions. Governments, therefore, should continue funding fusion research, while allocating resources to the installation of other available clean-energy sources.
